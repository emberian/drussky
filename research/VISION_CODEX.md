# Russian Workshop
*A language to think with, a voice to use, a library to enter*

I would build Ember a **workshop for making and understanding meaning**. Its central experience would be taking something worth saying, discovering how Russian expresses it, and using that machinery in a different situation.

The app would respect two facts simultaneously: Ember can understand an intricate explanation quickly, and unfamiliar sounds and endings can still be difficult to hold in mind. Intellectual depth and practice granularity would therefore be independent controls. A lesson could explain grammatical aspect seriously while asking for a three-word answer.

Six months would have a concrete destination: sustained reading of selected original Russian with increasingly selective help, alongside useful household conversation and connected writing. Reading an entire demanding novel comfortably would remain a longer ambition. The app would make the route enjoyable before that ambition is fulfilled.

## The workbench and its loop

The basic unit would be a **meaningful situation**, with three connected representations:

- **World:** what happened, what somebody wants, or what a claim means.
- **Russian:** the utterance expressing it.
- **Sound:** how somebody actually says it.

Ember could move between them. Hear an instruction and change a scene. Inspect a sentence and draw its participant relationships. Change an intention and say the resulting message. Read an argument and construct a counterexample.

The core loop would be:

**Encounter → inspect → change → express → meet again.**

For example, two people are arranging a room. Ember hears where a book belongs, moves it, then tells the other person where a different object is. A brief inspection reveals how location differs from destination. Later, that distinction returns in a household exchange and a spacecraft scene.

These would be adult experiments with consequences, without points, avatars, or rewards. The result of understanding an instruction is that the objects end up where intended.

Four destinations would organize the interface:

**Desk.** One current purpose, the exact unfinished activity, and three useful choices: continue, do a short session, or explore. “Explain where things are” is a purpose; “finish unit seven” is administrative information.

**Workbench.** A situation or utterance occupies the center. A persistent side panel holds vocabulary, an optional construction frame, and replay controls. “Show the machinery” opens grammatical structure without replacing the task.

**Library.** Continuous texts and recordings, with saved positions, contextual help, and optional discussion. Reading never requires completing the Workbench first.

**Notebook.** Ember’s writing, recordings, useful phrases, questions, and dated demonstrations of progress. It is a personal archive rather than the template through which every activity must pass.

On iPad, the Pencil can annotate any representation: circle an ending, connect a pronoun to its referent, sketch a scene, write a reply. On iPhone, the same activity becomes a compact listening or speaking interaction. The app preserves its purpose across devices rather than squeezing an entire paper page onto a phone.

## A day and a week

I would start with five 45-minute weekdays and one 60-minute weekend session: about 4¾ hours weekly, or roughly 124 scheduled hours over six months. Household conversation adds valuable experience when welcome; the plan remains usable without it.

A normal day has this shape:

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 5 minutes | Retrieve a few useful phrases or distinctions from previous days |
| 8 minutes | Explore one construction or sound distinction |
| 14 minutes | Read or listen to something continuous |
| 10 minutes | Complete an exchange with changing information |
| 8 minutes | Write something personal, revise it, or finish the current scene |

These are allocations, not five compulsory screens. A compelling reading session can absorb the whole period. The planner protects weekly balance instead of enforcing identical daily worksheets.

The twelve-minute version contains one brief retrieval, one comprehensible encounter, and one meaningful response. It generates no catch-up debt. After an absence, Ember resumes with a small sample and a useful new encounter, not a wall of overdue cards.

Each week develops one communicative purpose and one intellectual thread. An early week might pair “locate things and ask for help” with a story about contradictory directions. Later, “report what happened and what remains unfinished” connects household plans to narrative aspect.

Monday introduces the central contrast. Tuesday varies participants and vocabulary. Wednesday emphasizes unfamiliar voices. Thursday changes the situation enough to require choosing the construction. Friday uses it in a message or conversation. The weekend provides a longer reading session, a short independent sample, and a look at what should happen next.

The housemate’s role is ordinary companionship. The app might suggest, “Find out what they plan to cook,” and teach the necessary repair phrases beforehand. Nobody receives recording assignments or grading duties. Afterwards, Ember can save something they wanted to say but could not.

## Grammar: build a usable system

I would make explicit grammar a major strength.

Within the first few sessions, Ember gets a compact map of Russian: sound and spelling, word classes, agreement, cases, verb person and tense, aspect, motion, and clause structure. Every region is explorable immediately. The map distinguishes **having an explanation available** from **being able to use it**.

There would be no requirement to discover every rule through guessing. “Explain directly” and “let me work it out” are equally legitimate approaches.

The synthetic workbench operates on bounded constructions. Consider:

> Я читаю книгу.  
> I am reading a book.

Ember can change the reader, the object, or the time. The interface highlights which forms change and preserves the meaning alongside them. A compact paradigm is available beside the task. Once the relationship is understood, the table can close and the same operation becomes spoken or written retrieval.

A lesson would then break the comfortable pattern: hear a new sentence and identify its meaning; select between plausible constructions; express a new intention without a supplied Russian frame.

The representation beneath this is a small semantic structure—participants, event, time, polarity, relevant discourse context—linked to reviewed Russian realizations. It is useful for constructing examples and explaining dependencies. It is **not a universal compiler from English intentions into Russian**. Lexical preferences, aspect, information structure, idioms, and register prevent that fiction.

For each construction, the app teaches its productive scope and a nearby boundary. Aspect begins with concrete contrasts in context, then complicates them; it never becomes “perfective means finished.” Word families reveal useful relationships without implying every prefix produces a predictable meaning.

The sequence starts with reference, identification, possession, requests, location, and ordinary actions. It adds past events, plans, quantities, reasons, comparisons, and connected clauses. Reading recognition of participles can develop well ahead of comfortable production.

After concentrated introduction, practice mixes confusable alternatives. There is experimental support for interleaved grammar practice on delayed tests, although that does not establish spontaneous Russian conversation gains. [Nakata and Suzuki’s study](https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12581)

Working-memory support remains external: persistent instructions, visible referents, short audio segments, and optional frames. The difficulty should lie in the linguistic choice being learned.

## Speaking: have something to accomplish

Speaking starts immediately at the learner’s current level. The first session samples what Ember already acquired during these two weeks; it does not restart an alphabet course automatically.

Three kinds of speaking remain distinct.

**Sound practice** connects a model, an articulatory explanation, listening discrimination, and a short production. Stress, consonant softness, vowel reduction, and troublesome clusters receive recurring attention. A single useful comparison beats a screen full of acoustic graphs.

**Formulation practice** starts from a scene or intention. “Ask whether there is any tea.” A phrase frame is available, but the first attempt can happen without it. The next turn changes the substance: coffee is unavailable; ask for water instead.

**Conversation** involves information the learner does not already possess. The partner has a different schedule, knows where an object is, or disagrees about an interpretation. The learner must listen and respond.

The conversational agent receives a scenario, private facts, communicative goals, and limits on complexity. It does not receive a mandate to accept everything Ember says. It can ask for clarification or respond to the meaning actually expressed. During assessment, its hidden information must remain hidden from the scorer’s interpretation of what the learner communicated.

Feedback defaults to one consequential correction after the turn. If the intended message is unclear, repair that first. If it is clear, select a useful linguistic refinement and continue. Detailed correction remains available.

Assessment asks whether the task succeeded, which ideas were conveyed, what clarification was needed, and how much support was used. A matching transcript establishes neither accent quality nor effortless speaking. The app preserves audio and offers a narrower conclusion: “The recognizer recovered these words,” or “The simulated partner understood the requested time.”

Near-native pronunciation can remain an aspiration. Progress toward it needs actual listening judgments and validated acoustic evidence, beyond successful ASR.

## Listening: understand before transcribing

Listening would have its own substantial curriculum.

Early tasks include choosing the intended object, identifying who needs help, following a short instruction, and distinguishing a question from a statement in context. Later tasks include voice messages, interruptions, corrections, short accounts, and disagreement.

Dictation is useful, but it combines hearing with spelling and memory. It cannot represent the whole listening strand.

A listening screen initially shows the situation and question, with Play, Replay, and Show text. During teaching, captions are welcome. During an independent sample, the first response precedes transcript revelation. The app records whether understanding followed one hearing, repetition, slower playback, or text.

Material moves from clear short turns toward connected speech with varied speakers. It introduces variation deliberately rather than adding background noise as a generic difficulty knob. At least some later samples use unheard human voices; synthetic voice changes alone cannot establish generalization to people.

After a misunderstanding, the app reveals the crucial span, explains what changed the meaning, and replays the whole utterance. Then it offers another message requiring the same distinction.

Weekly assessment uses several short unheard messages. Ember may answer in English, manipulate a scene, or give a brief Russian reply. Those response modes answer different questions and remain separate in the record.

## Writing: leave increasingly substantial traces

Writing has three strands: script, spelling, and composition.

Script instruction is compact and responsive. Demonstrate a useful hand-printed form, copy it, retrieve it, use it in a word. Introduce cursive reading through real notes and common ambiguous shapes; offer cursive production when Ember wants it. Adult new-script research supports including handwriting, without establishing that handwriting every exercise is best. [Wiley and Rapp](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34184564/)

Russian keyboard practice begins early as another practical skill.

Composition grows from labels and requests into messages, accounts, descriptions, and arguments. Prompts have recipients and purposes: explain an absence, describe a strange event, object to a claim, summarize a paragraph.

The correction screen preserves the original ink or typed text. It separates:

- whether the intended message came through;
- a grammatical or lexical problem;
- spelling and punctuation;
- optional stylistic improvement.

A more elegant rewrite is never silently presented as proof that the original was wrong. Handwriting recognition asks for confirmation when uncertain.

After correction, Ember writes a changed message later. Recopying the corrected paragraph remains useful practice, but it cannot demonstrate independent composition.

A weekly sample is written before assistance. Assessment tracks ideas communicated, organization, and errors relative to actual opportunities to use the targeted construction. Avoiding a difficult construction should not look like mastering it.

## Reading begins now

The Library has two simultaneous routes.

The **easy shelf** supplies continuous, understandable Russian: recurring conversations, speculative fiction, short explanations, letters, and accounts. Its job is volume and momentum. Most reading ends with continuation, not a quiz.

The **wanted shelf** contains original science fiction, mathematics, and philosophy selected with Ember. These texts are accessible immediately with substantial support. The app does not hide them behind a vocabulary gate.

A reading screen offers the original, optional stress marks, sentence audio, contextual glosses, and paragraph translation. Selecting a phrase gives its meaning here before morphology. “Show the structure” reveals clause boundaries, referents, and dependencies. An adaptation occupies a separate view and remains visibly distinct from the source.

The most important interaction is often a question about the idea. What changed in the scene? Which condition does the conclusion require? Does the author assert this, or report somebody else’s view?

English answers are welcome when assessing comprehension. Russian expression receives separate coaching.

Mathematics retains notation. Ember can draw a counterexample, change a hypothesis, or match a Russian statement to a finite structure. Familiarity with a theorem must not supply the answer automatically. Philosophy distinguishes understanding an argument from agreeing with it. Literary reading allows ambiguity and multiple defensible interpretations.

Only a few expressions per session enter deliberate review. Lookup is cheap; review obligations are expensive.

## The six-month bridge

The schedule below is an intended progression, adjusted to observed performance rather than elapsed time alone.

| Period | Main development | Reading experience |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–4 | Consolidate decoding; household exchanges; basic reference, questions, location, possession, present actions | Short connected scenes and explanations; small original passages with generous help |
| Weeks 5–8 | Past events, plans, quantities, common case constructions, expanding verb forms | Recurring stories of several hundred words; simple mathematical definitions and explanations |
| Weeks 9–12 | Aspect contrasts, reasons, comparisons, relative clauses, retelling | Longer episodes; original narrative paragraphs; reconstructing short arguments |
| Weeks 13–18 | Clause relationships, motion in context, reading recognition of participles and passive constructions | Sustained original sections; familiar-topic mathematical exposition; selected philosophical passages |
| Weeks 19–26 | Consolidation around chosen works; vocabulary, register, and discourse specific to those works | A continuing original reading project, with help concentrated on genuine obstacles |

I would budget content production for roughly 50,000–80,000 running words encountered over six months, including rereading, with new and repeated text counted separately. That is a supply and exposure target, not a competence threshold.

The endpoint is a **reading portfolio**: several sustained original science-fiction sections, a mathematical exposition understood through its dependencies, and a philosophical passage whose claims and qualifications Ember can explain. Alongside these, unfamiliar easier passages should be readable with substantially less help.

A monthly fresh passage checks whether progress extends beyond memorized material. Record central meaning, important relations, assistance, and effort. Repeatedly needing full translation is useful information for changing the next month’s instruction.

I would not replace Drussky’s existing coverage thresholds with supposedly authoritative higher ones. Lexical coverage helps select material; it does not certify comprehension. Replication research makes the limitations of universal thresholds particularly clear. [Kremmel and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12622)

## Producing a course worth trusting

The content system begins with a human-readable, 24-week curriculum specification: communicative purposes, construction contrasts, lexical priorities, sound targets, and reading destinations. AI expands this into a substantial library of related encounters.

A weekly pack contains continuous texts, dialogues, construction examples, listening tasks, production situations, and reserved assessment variants. Items store intended meaning, prerequisites, acceptable alternatives, feedback, exact audio identity, and source relationships.

Production has four stages:

1. **Draft:** generate within explicit linguistic and curricular constraints.
2. **Critique:** another model checks Russian, naturalness, stress, meaning, ambiguity, and whether the task tests its intended distinction.
3. **Validate:** deterministic tools check schemas, references, answer leakage, morphology where reliable, audio availability, and source fidelity.
4. **Preview:** inspect rendered activities and actual audio, including the wrong-answer experience.

Model agreement is a review signal, not native certification. Persistent disagreement triggers revision or replacement. A modest commissioned review by a qualified Russian teacher would be valuable for the foundational spine and sampled feedback, but everyday learning must not depend on finding a human approver.

Audio QA checks what was spoken, stress-sensitive words, truncation, question/statement distinctions, and correspondence with the displayed sentence. Round-trip transcription catches some failures; it cannot certify pronunciation.

Original sources retain exact text, edition information, and provenance. Adaptations and translations have separate identities. Correcting content creates a new version and repairs affected feedback or learner evidence without rewriting the historical attempt.

The expensive work happens ahead of study. Ember should rarely wait for a model to invent the next exercise.

## The learner model: useful uncertainty

I would retain an append-only evidence history, but narrow the authority of everything inferred from it.

The model has three layers:

**Observations:** what was presented, heard, revealed, attempted, and judged.

**Predictions:** how likely a particular kind of retrieval is to succeed under specified conditions.

**Teaching decisions:** what useful activity fits the current purpose, time, and support preference.

A record binds the exact stimulus version, exposure family, response, original ink or audio, assistance chronology, target distinction, assessor, and assessment dimension. Recognition hypotheses remain distinguishable from the learner’s confirmed response. Corrections can supersede judgments while preserving originals.

Memory scheduling applies to useful retrieval targets: lexical senses, phrases, inflected forms, and constructions in particular tasks. FSRS is a reasonable starting scheduler. It does not become a general theory of understanding.

Knowing a written form does not establish hearing it. Understanding a construction does not establish producing it. Successful sentence completion does not independently credit every component. Related evidence can motivate a prediction, but the app labels that inference.

The user-facing summary says:

> You followed four of six new spoken directions without text. Two needed replay. You used the location construction in three new written descriptions; spontaneous speaking needs another sample.

It does not say “Russian: 37% mastered.”

The planner follows explicit priorities: preserve meaningful input, maintain selected useful memories, revisit recurring obstacles, and advance the current purpose. Review has a time budget. A large backlog reduces new deliberate memorization rather than confiscating reading.

I would remove automatic “tired,” “sharp,” and preferred-hour conclusions. Ember can select “less to hold in mind,” “more explanation,” or “stretch me.” Timing and error patterns may suggest offering a shorter session, without claiming to diagnose a mental state.

Assessment variants have tracked exposure ancestry. A paraphrase of yesterday’s answer is not automatically an unseen test. The model’s success is improved teaching and better predictions on fresh attempts, not a beautifully populated graph.

## Hardware and machine learning

I would build native Apple clients around SwiftUI, PencilKit, and coordinated audio capture and playback. The iPad is the main workbench; the iPhone handles brief listening, speech, and capture; the Mac supports long reading, typing, and content preparation.

The Mac produces weekly packs, synthesizes audio, aligns recordings, runs batch review, and caches expensive explanations. Generation is bounded and resumable. A prepared week works offline.

Cloud models provide sophisticated explanation, open-ended feedback, and flexible dialogue. Claude, Gemini, and Codex access would be used according to measured task performance, rather than permanent provider mythology. One model can draft and another critique; disagreement remains visible to the production system.

On-device ML handles recognition and narrow assistance where it performs adequately. I would not entrust Russian grammar correction to a small model merely because it runs locally; the repository’s own [on-device evaluation](/Users/ember/dev/rufren/research/ONDEVICE_TUTOR.md) supplies ample reason for caution.

Pronunciation analysis remains an instrument supporting comparison until validated on relevant learner speech. Offline failure leaves a usable model, preserved attempt, and pending feedback.

Sync preserves drafts, original artifacts, and stable identities. Export and tested restoration are core features: six months of somebody’s emerging Russian deserves durable storage.

## What survives Drussky

I would keep Drussky’s affection for the learner’s own traces: ink, recordings, diary entries, and the ability to revisit an earlier self. I would keep contextual help, reusable audio with provenance, sound contrasts, morphology resources, modality-specific retrieval, and LearningCore’s replayable history. The native Apple direction fits this learner well.

I would throw away the writing-majority rule, compulsory school marks, the assumption that every experience belongs on a numbered exercise page, automatic root mastery, and “distance to your book” presented as a measure of reading readiness. I would also discard a single narrator as the learner’s whole auditory world.

I would preserve the notebook as an artifact while changing the app’s center of gravity.

The largest difference is **productive control over meaning**. Ember would repeatedly discover that changing an ending, aspect, word order, or connective changes what another person understands. The grammar workbench makes those relationships inspectable; conversation and reading make them necessary.

The principal uncertainty is whether explicit structural understanding will become rapid access during actual use. The design answers that uncertainty with abundant comprehensible encounters, repeated formulation, and fresh situations—not increasingly elaborate explanations alone.

The app succeeds when Ember reaches for Russian to ask something, describe something, or find out what happens next, and has enough language available to continue.
